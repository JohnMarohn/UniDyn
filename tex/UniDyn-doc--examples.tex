\subsection{Two spins}
% ---------------------------
\label{sec:two-spins}

Define two spins with angular momentum operators $\bm{I}$ and $\bm{S}$.
The first spin has an unspecified total angular momentum while the second spin has total angular momentum $S = 1/2$. The two spins interact through the weak-coupling Hamiltonian ${\cal H} = J\, I_{z} S_{z}$.
The \verb+Evolver2+ algorithm computes
\begin{equation}
e^{-i \, J t \, I_z S_z} 
\: I_x \: 
e^{+i \, J \, I_z S_z} = 
  I_x \, \cos{(J t  \big/ 2 )} 
  + 2 I_y S_z \, \sin{(J t \big/ 2)},
\end{equation}
which is a non-trivial extension of the single-spin rotations encoded in the unit tests.
For the evolution of $S_x$, \verb+Evolver2+ computes
\begin{equation}
e^{-i \, J t \, I_z S_z} 
\: S_x \: 
e^{+i \, J \, I_z S_z} = 
  S_x \, \cos{\big( J t  \sqrt{I_{z}^2} \big)} 
  +  \frac{2 I_z S_y}{\sqrt{I_{z}^2}} \sin{\big(J t \sqrt{I_{z}^2}\big)}.
\label{eq:Sx-J-coupling-example}
\end{equation}
If we specify the spin angular momentum $I$ then it is straightforward to write down the eigenvalues of $I_z$ and expand the trigonometric operators in Eq.~\ref{eq:Sx-J-coupling-example} using L\"{o}dwin projection-operator theorem \cite{Lowdin1955mar}.

\subsection{Harmonic oscillator}
% ---------------------------------------
\label{sec:ho}

Consider a harmonic oscillator with Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
{\cal H}_0 = \omega_0 \left( a^{\dagger} a + \frac{1}{2} \right)
\end{equation}
and consider that we calculate oscillator dynamics in an interaction representation defined by ${\cal H}_0$.
The application of an on-resonance force leads to a Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
{\cal H}_1 = \frac{A}{\sqrt{2}} (e^{-i \phi} \, a^{\dagger} + e^{+i \phi} \, a)
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ is the phase of the oscillating force.
The \verb+Evolver2+ algorithm computes that the oscillator's position and momentum evolve under the action of ${\cal H}_1$ as follows: 
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
e^{-i \, {\cal H}_1  t}  Q e^{+i \, {\cal H}_1  t} & = Q + A t \sin{\phi} \\
e^{-i \, {\cal H}_1  t}  P e^{+i \, {\cal H}_1  t} & = P + A t \cos{\phi}.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
This example shows that the algorithm handles complex numbers correctly.

Modulating the spring constant of the oscillator at twice the oscillator's resonance frequency $2 \omega_0$ gives rise to a ``squeezing'' Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
{\cal H}_2 = \frac{\Omega}{2} \left( e^{-i \phi} (a^\dagger)^2 + e^{+i \phi} a^2 \right)
\end{equation}
with $\phi$ the phase of the oscillating spring constant.  
Under the action of this Hamiltonian, the \verb+Evolver2+ algorithm computes that
\begin{equation}
e^{-i \, {\cal H}_2  t}  a^{\dagger} e^{+i \, {\cal H}_2  t}
 = a^{\dagger} \cosh{(\Omega \, t)} - i a e^{i \phi} \sinh{(\Omega \, t)}.
\label{eq:sqeeze-a-dagger--resonance}
\end{equation}
When $\phi = \pi/2$, 
\begin{equation}
\{ Q, P \} \xrightarrow{{\cal H}_2}  \{ e^{-\Omega \, t} Q, e^{+\Omega \, t} P \} 
\end{equation}
we see that $Q$ is attenuated while $P$ is amplified.
On the other hand, when $\phi = 3 \pi/2$, 
\begin{equation}
\{ Q, P \} \xrightarrow{{\cal H}_2}  \{ e^{+\Omega \, t} Q, e^{-\Omega t} \, P \},
\end{equation}
and now $Q$ is amplified while $P$ is attenuated. 
For these two choices of phase, the expectation value of the product $Q P$ is conserved.
This example shows that the \verb+Evolver2+ algorithm can capture the physics of degenerate parametric amplification \cite{Mollow1967aug,Mollow1967auga}.

Consider the more complicated case of non-degenerate parametric amplification, when the spring constant is modulated off-resonance at frequency $2 \omega$.
To remove the time-dependence in the resulting Hamiltonian, we need an interaction representation defined by $\omega (a^{\dagger} a + 1/2)$.
\verb+Evolver2+ computes that, in this interaction representation, $a^{\dagger}$ evolves as follows:
\begin{multline}
e^{-i \, (\Delta \omega \, a^{\dagger} a +  {\cal H}_2)  t}  
a^{\dagger} 
e^{-i \, (\Delta \omega \, a^{\dagger} a +  {\cal H}_2)  t}  
\\
= a^{\dagger} \cos{(t \sqrt{\Delta \omega^2 - \Omega^2})}
   - \frac{i (\Delta \omega \, a^{\dagger} + \Omega \, e^{i \phi}  \, a) \sin{(t \sqrt{\Delta \omega^2 - \Omega^2})}}
            {\sqrt{\Delta \omega^2 - \Omega^2}}.
\label{eq:sqeeze-a-dagger--off-resonance}
\end{multline}
with $\Delta \omega = \omega_0 - \omega$ a resonance offset.
The Eq.~\ref{eq:sqeeze-a-dagger--off-resonance} calculation is challenging because $[\Delta \omega \, a^{\dagger} a, {\cal H}_2] \neq 0$, and the resulting operator transformation is non-trivial.
Equation~\ref{eq:sqeeze-a-dagger--off-resonance} reduces to Eq.~\ref{eq:sqeeze-a-dagger--resonance} in the on-resonance limit, $\Delta \omega \rightarrow 0$.
In the limit of no parametric amplification, $\Omega \rightarrow 0$, Eq.~\ref{eq:sqeeze-a-dagger--off-resonance} reduces to the expected $a^{\dagger} e^{-i \, \Delta \omega \, t}$.
In the general, off-resonance case, Eq.~\ref{eq:sqeeze-a-dagger--off-resonance} exhibits complicated dynamics ---  either oscillatory or exponential, depending on the relative size of $\Delta \omega$ and $\Omega$.

\subsection{Electron transfer}
% ------------------------------------
\label{sec:et}

In Ref.~\citenum{Mikkelsen1987feb}, electron transfer between two sites (H and H$^{+}$ for example) the electronic Hamiltonian, vibrational Hamiltonian, and electronic-vibrational coupling is treated using a Holstein molecular crystal model.
The electronic states are represented by an $I = 1/2$ spin system $\bm{I}$, and the vibrational states are represented by a harmonic oscillator with creation and annihilation operators $(a^{\dagger}, a)$.
To remove the electronic-vibrational coupling, dynamics are calculated in an interaction representation defined by an operator (in our notation)
\begin{equation}
T = -2 g I_z \big( a^{\dagger} - a \big).
\end{equation}
The associated unitary transformation corresponds to an electronic-state-dependent translation.
In this interaction representation, \verb+Evolver2+ correctly computes
\begin{equation}
e^{-i \, t \, T } \, I_{\pm} \, e^{+i \, t \, T } = e^{\pm 2 g  \big( a^{\dagger} - a \big)} \,  I_{\pm}
\label{eq:translate-T}
\end{equation}
This example shows that \verb+Evolver2+ can handle differential equations in which the coefficients are \emph{operators}.

This example highlights the limitations of \verb+Evolver1+, which fails to resolve Eq.~\ref{eq:translate-T}.
The first two derivatives in the $I_{+}$ case are
\begin{align}
\rho^{(0)} &= I_{+} \\
\rho^{(1)} &= 2 g \big( I_{+} a - I_{+} a^{\dagger} \big)
\end{align}
and the \verb+Divide+ function employed in \verb+Evolver1+ is unable to see that $\rho^{(1)}/\rho^{(0)} = 2 g  \big( a^{\dagger} - a \big)$.
The $\verb+Inv+$ function employed in \verb+Evolver2+ is able to implement this division correctly.

\subsection{Ion-based quantum computing}
% ------------------------------------------------------
\label{sec:qc}
%

%   /Homeworks solutions/10.2.pdf

% which looks at the case where the cavity is resonant with atom I but off resonance for atom S.

A pair of atoms placed in a single-mode electromagnetic cavity are described by the Tavis–Cummings Hamiltonian \cite{Tavis1968jun,Tavis1969dec}, ${\cal H} = {\cal H}_0 + {\cal H}_1$, with 
\begin{equation}
{\cal H}_0 = \omega_I I_z + \omega_S S_z + \omega a^{\dagger} a
\end{equation}
capturing the energy of the atoms and the cavity, and
\begin{equation}
{\cal H}_1 = g_I \omega \big( I_{+} a + I_{-} a^{\dagger} \big) + g_S \omega \big( S_{+} a + S_{-} a^{\dagger} \big)
\end{equation}
describing the exchange of photons between the atoms' electronic states and the cavity.
The above Hamiltonians approximate each atom as a two-level system whose electronic-state populations and coherences are computed using $I$- and $S$-spin pseudo angular-momentum operators. 
The frequencies $\omega_I$, $\omega_S$, $\omega$ are the transition frequencies of the $I$ atom, $S$ atom, and cavity, respectively, while $g_I$ and $g_S$ are two unitless coupling coefficients describing the atom-field coupling.

Let us assume that the atoms are of the same species and let the cavity be detuned from each atom by an amount $\Delta$:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
\omega_I & = \omega - \Delta \\
\omega_S & = \omega - \Delta 
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
To compute the effect of the Hamiltonian ${\cal H}_1$ on the system, first transform ${\cal H}_1$ into an interaction representation defined by ${\cal H}_0$.  We obtain, using \verb+Evolver2+,
\begin{align}
\widetilde{\cal H}_{1}(t)
  & = e^{-i t {\cal H}_0} {\cal H}_1 e^{+i t {\cal H}_0} \\
  & = g_I  \left( e^{-i t \Delta} I_{-} a^{\dagger} + e^{+i t \Delta} I_{+} a \right)
        + g_S \left( e^{-i t \Delta} S_{-} a^{\dagger} + e^{+i t \Delta} S_{+} a \right)
\end{align}
The transformed interaction Hamiltonian is time-dependent.
We can approximate its effect on the atoms and the cavity using the Magnus expansion \cite{Magnus1954nov,Blanes2009jan}.
The zeroth-order term in the expansion is
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\cal H}_{1}^{(0)}
 = \frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{c}}} \int_{0}^{t_{\mathrm{c}}} \widetilde{\cal H}_{1}(t) \, dt = 0
\end{equation}
with $t_{\mathrm{c}}$ a cycle time chosen to be the period, $2 \pi \big/ \Delta$, associated with the detuning frequency $
\Delta$.
To zeroth order, the interaction has no effect. 
The first-order term is found to be
\begin{align}
\widetilde{\cal H}_{1}^{(1)}
 & = - \frac{i}{2 t_{\mathrm{c}}} 
    \int_{0}^{t_{\mathrm{c}}}  \left( \int_{0}^{t_2} [\widetilde{\cal H}_{1}(t_1), \widetilde{\cal H}_{1}(t_2)] \, d t_1 \right) \, d t_2 \\
 & = - \frac{\omega^2}{\Delta} 
 \left( 
   2 g_I^2 \big( n + \frac{1}{2} \big) I_z 
   + 2 g_S^2 \big( n + \frac{1}{2} \big) S_z 
   + g_I g_S \big( I_{-} S^{+} + I_{+} S^{-} \big)
 \right)
 \label{eq:ion-H11}
\end{align}
with $n$ the number of photons in the cavity and $t_{\mathrm{c}} = 2 \pi \big/ \Delta$.
In writing the above result, we have dropped a trivial constant term.
The first two terms in eq.~\ref{eq:ion-H11} correspond to a photon-number-dependent shift of each atom's electronic transition frequency.
The last two terms represent a cavity-mediated exchange interaction.
With $n = 0$ photons in the cavity and with both coupling constants identical, $g_I = g$ and  $g_S = g$, 
using \verb+Evolver2+ we can compute the effect of the first-order term on an initial density operator $\rho(0) \propto I_{+}$ analytically:
\begin{align}
\rho(t) 
  & \propto e^{-i t \widetilde{\cal H}_{1}^{(1)}} I_{+} \, e^{+i t \widetilde{\cal H}_{1}^{(1)}} \\
  & = e^{- i t \Omega} \big(  I_{+} \cos{(\Omega t)} + I_z S_{+} \sin{(\Omega t)} \big) 
\end{align}
with $\Omega = g^2 \omega^2 \big/ \Delta$ an effective Rabi frequency.
The second term, in magnetic resonance language, is an anti-phase single quantum coherence between the $I$ states and the $S$ states.  
Starting instead with both atoms in the ground state, $\rho(0) = (\frac{1}{2} + I_z )(\frac{1}{2} + S_z )$, let us apply a brief $\pi/2$ pulse to each atomic system.
These pulses will transform the density operator to $\rho(0^{+}) = (\frac{1}{2} + I_x )(\frac{1}{2} + S_x)$. 
Evolving $\rho(0^{+})$ under the eq.~\ref{eq:ion-H11} Hamiltonian for a time $\Delta t = \pi \big/ 2 \Omega$, the density operator is now
\[
  \rho(\Delta t) = \frac{1}{4} + I_x S_z + I_y S_y + I_z S_x, 
\]
The value of the Wootters' concurrence function \cite{Wootters1998mar,Rungta2001sep} for this density operator is 1; the two atoms are maximally entangled.
The associated wavefunction is the Bell-like state \cite{Horodecki2009jun}
\[
  \frac{1}{2} \left(  \ket{\uparrow \uparrow} 
    - \ket{\uparrow \downarrow} 
    - \ket{\downarrow \uparrow} 
    - \ket{\downarrow \downarrow} \right).
\]
Zheng and Guo \cite{Zheng2000sep} developed a similar entanglement scheme by evolving the wavefunction $\psi_0 = \ket{\uparrow \downarrow}$; their cavity-assisted entanglement scheme was demonstrated experimentally by Osnaghi and coworkers \cite{Osnaghi2001jun}.
Here we have recapitulated Zheng and Guo's work succinctly using the Magnus expansion to create an effective Hamiltonian and evolving the density operator using this effective Hamiltonian.

\subsection{Electron spin resonance: an $N$-spin signal expression with $N\to\infty$} %Density operator expression for an $N$-spin system with $N \to \infty$}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 
\label{sec:esr}
Double quantum coherence (DQC) is a major pulsed electron spin resonance (ESR) technique for measuring inter-spin distances in the range of 10 to 80 \AA\, typically in doubly spin-labeled proteins in the frozen state \cite{Saxena1997aug,Borbat1999nov,SinhaRoy2026jan}. In its standard form, DQC is a 6-pulse experiment, described in Figure~\ref{fig:dqc}. For an isolated pair of spin-1/2s, double quantum coherence is generated selectively using the $\pi/2 \text{--} t_\mathrm{p} \text{--} \pi \text{--} t_\mathrm{p} \text{--} \pi/2$-section of the pulse sequence, combined with phase cycling. The middle section, $t_1 \text{--} \pi \text{--} t_1$, refocuses the DQC and then the last $\pi/2$-pulse converts it to anti-phase single quantum coherence (SQC). The final $t_2 \text{--} \pi \text{--} t_2$-sequence creates in-phase SQC and a refocused echo is obtained at $t_2 = t_\mathrm{m} \text{ (constant) } - t_\mathrm{p}$ (cf. Figure~\ref{fig:dqc}). 

For an isolated spin pair with weak dipolar coupling (spin-spin distance $\ge 17$ \AA) and with ideal pulse approximation, the DQC signal expression is given by \cite{Borbat1999nov}

\begin{equation}
    V_{0} = \frac{i}{8} \, s_{a_{12}}^{t_\mathrm{p}}\,s_{a_{12}}^{t_2}
    \label{eqn:eq-dqcideal}
\end{equation}
where $s_a^t \equiv \sin{(a\, t)}$. The dipolar coupling constant $a_{12}$ is given by \cite{Borbat1999nov}

\begin{equation}
    a_{12} = \omega_0\,(1-3\,\cos^2{\theta_{12}})/r_{12}^3  
    \label{eqn:eq-dipCoup}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_0$ is the dipolar constant, $r_{12}$ is the distance between the spin pair and $\theta_{12}$ is the angle between the $\overrightarrow{r}_{12}$ vector and the external magnetic field. The signal from a frozen sample gives a powder average over $\theta_{12}$ and thus, inter-spin distance $r_{12}$ is measured by determining $a_{12}$ from DQC signals. In biophysical applications, with doubly spin-labeled proteins, such distance information can help predict protein structures and structural changes \cite{Borbat1999nov,SinhaRoy2026jan}.  

However, in any doubly spin-labeled protein samples, the intermolecular dipolar interactions produce a non-negligible background signal or baseline. As a result, an accurate DQC analysis to obtain meaningful information requires a robust and accurate theory for baseline subtraction. This remained a key challenge in DQC data analysis since its development in the late 1990s.

\begin{figure}[htbp!]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/DQC_Figure.png}
\caption{DQC 6-pulse sequence with the short and tall rectangles representing $\pi/2$ and $\pi$-pulses, respectively. Typically, $t_\mathrm{m} = t_\mathrm{p} + t_2$ is kept constant in an experiment, while varying $t_p$ from 0 to $t_\mathrm{m}$ ($t_2$ = $t_\mathrm{m} - t_\mathrm{p}$); $t_1$ is kept constant as well. The pulse phases ($\phi_j$) and the propagators, for each pulse ($R$) and free-evolution ($Q$), are shown. Starting from the equilibrium density operator $\rho_0$, the overall density operator expressions are derived in four stages: preparation of multiple quantum coherences ($\rho_\mathrm{A}$), refocusing of DQC ($\rho_\mathrm{B}$), conversion of DQC to single quantum coherence ($\rho_\mathrm{C}$) and formation of refocused echo ($\rho_\mathrm{D}$).}
\label{fig:dqc}
\end{figure}

In a recent work, Roy, Marohn, and co workers used \verb+Evolver1+ to obtain analytical and symbolic expressions of $\rho_\mathrm{A}$, $\rho_\mathrm{B}$, $\rho_\mathrm{C}$ and $\rho_\mathrm{D}$ (cf. Figure~\ref{fig:dqc}) with multiple spin-1/2 particles. In this work, the total Hamiltonian (${\cal H}$) is defined as \cite{Slichter1990,SinhaRoy2024apr}
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        {\cal H} &= {\cal H}_\mathrm{S} + {\cal H}_\mathrm{B} + {\cal H}_\mathrm{SB} \\
        {\cal H}_\mathrm{S} &=\, \Omega_1\,I_\mathrm{1z}\,+\,\Omega_2\,I_\mathrm{2z}\,+\,a_{12}\,I_\mathrm{1z}\,I_\mathrm{2z} \\
        {\cal H}_\mathrm{B} &=\,\sum_{j=3}^{N}\Omega_{j}\,I_{j\mathrm{z}}\,+\,\sum_{i=3}^{N-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^N{a_{ij}\,I_{i\mathrm{z}}\,I_{j\mathrm{z}}} \\
        {\cal H}_\mathrm{SB} &= \,\sum_{j=3}^N{\left(a_{1j}\,I_\mathrm{1z}\,I_{j\mathrm{z}} + a_{2j}\,I_\mathrm{2z}\,I_{j\mathrm{z}}\right)}
    \end{split}
    \label{eqn:eq-dqcHamiltonian}
\end{equation}
where ${\cal H}_\mathrm{S}$, ${\cal H}_\mathrm{B}$ and ${\cal H}_\mathrm{SB}$ are the Hamiltonians for an intramolecular spin pair, the spin bath and the interactions between the intramolecular pair and the bath, respectively. The resonance offset for the $j$th spin and the dipolar coupling constant between the $\{i, j\}$-pair of spins are given by $\Omega_j$ and $a_{ij}$, respectively.

Starting with an isolated spin pair ($N_{\mathrm{spin}}$=2), phase-cycled analytical expression of $\rho_\mathrm{A}$ was obtained for up to $N_{\mathrm{spin}}$=9, i.e., the intramolecular pair plus 7 bath spins \cite{SinhaRoy2024apr}. Given that $N_{\mathrm{spin}} = 9$ is near the limit of what can be simulated \emph{numerically}, we were initially surprised to find that the evolution of so many spins could be treated \emph{analytically}. We should consider, however, that the DQC pulse sequence is relatively simple and that spin-spin couplings act in a pairwise fashion \cite{SinhaRoy2024apr}. These constraints limited the terms in the density operator to many fewer than would be included in a numerical calculation. 

The patterns obtained from these expressions with increasing $N_{\mathrm{spin}}$ were used to express $\rho_\mathrm{A}$ for an arbitrary $N_{\mathrm{spin}}$ = $N$. Subsequently, the general $N$-spin expressions of $\rho_\mathrm{B}$ through $\rho_\mathrm{D}$ were obtained. Given the symmetry of the problem and with the high temperature approximation, it was sufficient to derive the density operator expressions starting with an equilibrium density operator $\rho_0 \propto I_\mathrm{1z}$ \cite{Slichter1990}. Finally, we collect all the terms that contain $a_{12}$ (i.e., the intramolecular dipolar contribution) and denote the collection as $\rho_\mathrm{D,DQC}$; the rest of the terms were collected under $\rho_\mathrm{D,BG}$. The following expressions of $\rho_\mathrm{D,DQC}$ and $\rho_\mathrm{D,BG}$ were obtained \cite{SinhaRoy2024apr} 

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \rho_\mathrm{D,DQC} &= \frac{i}{4}I_\mathrm{1-}\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\binom{N-2}{n}(-1)^{n}\prod_{j=2}^{n+2}s_{a_{1j}}^{t_p}s_{a_{1j}}^{t_2}\prod_{k=n+3}^{N}c_{a_{1k}}^{t_p}c_{a_{1k}}^{t_2} \\
        \rho_\mathrm{D,BG} &= -\frac{i}{4}I_\mathrm{1-}\sum_{n=1}^{N-2}\binom{N-2}{n}\,(-1)^{n}\prod_{j=3}^{n+2}s_{a_{1j}}^{t_p}s_{a_{1j}}^{t_2}\prod_{k=n+3}^{N}c_{a_{1k}}^{t_p}c_{a_{1k}}^{t_2} \\
    \end{split}
    \label{eqn:eq-rhoD}
\end{equation}

The $N$-spin signal expression $V$ was obtained using the relations $V = \mathrm{Tr}(\rho_\mathrm{D}\cdot I_{1+})$ and $\mathrm{Tr}(I_{1-}\cdot I_{1+})/\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{1}_{2^N})$=1/2, given by \cite{SinhaRoy2024apr}
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        V_\mathrm{DQC} &= \underbrace{\frac{i}{8}s_{a_{12}}^{t_p}s_{a_{12}}^{t_2}}_{V_0}\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\binom{N-2}{n}(-1)^{n}\prod_{j=3}^{n+2}s_{a_{1j}}^{t_p}s_{a_{1j}}^{t_2}\prod_{k=n+3}^{N}c_{a_{1k}}^{t_p}c_{a_{1k}}^{t_2} \\
        V_\mathrm{BG} &= -\frac{i}{8}\sum_{n=1}^{N-2}\binom{N-2}{n}(-1)^{n}\prod_{j=3}^{n+2}s_{a_{1j}}^{t_p}s_{a_{1j}}^{t_2}\prod_{k=n+3}^{N}c_{a_{1k}}^{t_p}c_{a_{1k}}^{t_2}    
    \end{split}
    \label{eqn:eq-dqcNspin}
\end{equation}

We recognized that in a homogeneous sample, we can implement the following replacement rules to any intermolecular spin pairs 
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        & a_{1k} \to a(r, \theta) \\
        & \prod_{k=1}^n \sin{(a_{1k}\, t)} \to \sin^n{(a(r, \theta)\,t)} \\
        & \prod_{k=1}^n \cos{(a_{1k}\, t)} \to \cos^n{(a(r, \theta)\,t)}
    \end{split}
    \label{eqn:eq-replacement}
\end{equation}
Subsequently, Eq.~\ref{eqn:eq-dqcNspin} was rewritten using the replacement rules in Eq.~\ref{eqn:eq-replacement} and remarkably, these results could be averaged over the entire range of $r$ and $\theta$ and extrapolated to obtain analytical expressions for the average intermolecular contribution valid in the $N_{\mathrm{spin}} \rightarrow \infty$ limit. 

The following closed form expressions for the DQC and background signals were obtained \cite{SinhaRoy2024apr}
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        &V_\mathrm{DQC} = C\, V_0\,\exp{\left(-4\,C\,\eta\,t_\mathrm{m}\right)} \\
        &V_\mathrm{BG} = \frac{i\,C}{8}\,\bigg[\exp{\left(-2\,C\,\eta\,(t_\mathrm{m} + |t_\xi|)\right)} - \exp{\left(-4\,C\,\eta\,t_\mathrm{m}\right)}\bigg] \\
    \end{split}
    \label{eqn:eq-dqcCF}
\end{equation}
where $t_\mathrm{m} = t_\mathrm{p} + t_2$, $t_\xi = t_\mathrm{p} - t_2$, $\eta = 4\,\pi^2\,\omega_0/9\,\sqrt{3}$ (a constant) and $C$ is the doubly spin-labeled protein concentration.

Interestingly, replacing $C$ by an effective concentration to account for finite pulses that only partially cover the frequency bandwidth of the spin labels, simulations using Eq.~\ref{eqn:eq-dqcCF} matched experimental results very well \cite{SinhaRoy2026jan, Mandato2025jul}.

Further applications of \verb+Evolver1+, including the effects of the pseudo-secular dipolar coupling and finite pulses to the intramolecular DQC and other pulsed dipolar electron spin resonance techniques, have resulted into significantly improved, yet rapid, data analysis \cite{SinhaRoy2024apr,SinhaRoy2025jan,SinhaRoy2026jan}.
